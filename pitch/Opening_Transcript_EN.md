# Pitch opening: speaker script

3 slides, about 2–3 minutes at a normal pace. The script is also in the PowerPoint speaker notes.

## Slide 1 | Her body is changing; understanding and support haven't kept up

Imagine a woman in her forties. She goes to work, buys groceries and plays tennis with friends as usual, but lately she has started sleeping badly and finds it harder to concentrate. She can feel that something is changing, but she may not know what is happening.

Research makes this gap concrete: in one US survey, 34% of participants were unsure which reproductive stage they were in; in a survey of mostly UK residents, only about one in five felt they understood the menopause transition well; and in another US company survey, only 9% of those with current symptoms said they had received any workplace support.

These numbers come from different samples, but together they remind us: the body is already changing, while understanding and support may not keep up.

## Slide 2 | Perimenopause: changes can begin before periods stop

First, one word needs explaining: perimenopause. In everyday speech people often just say “menopause” or “the change,” which is a broad, loose term.

For most women, natural menopause happens between 45 and 55, but changes can start earlier. Perimenopause begins when signs of the menopausal transition appear and lasts until one full year after the final period; menopause itself is the point confirmed in hindsight, after twelve months in a row without a period, once other causes are ruled out.

During this transition, hormones fluctuate and eventually decline, which can bring changes in periods, sleep, temperature regulation or mood. Everyone's experience is different, and we can't blame every bout of tiredness or every bad night on perimenopause.

## Slide 3 | Her life is already busy; health support should fit into it more easily

Knowing this, we asked: can health support fit more naturally into the life she is already living?

She may wear a wearable at night, shop during the day, and already have tennis with friends on her calendar. Our idea: with her permission, connect this existing information, spot trends worth attention, do one very light check-in only when it's really needed, and then offer one small, evidence-based, optional action.

We want to reduce the work of searching, filtering and making sense of health information. This is the product hypothesis we want to test: she already has plenty of data and plenty to do, and we help her find one small thing worth doing for her health today.
